<h2>What the research actually says</h2>
<p>Most of the business-card statistics circulating online cannot be traced to a study. We are not going to repeat them. There is one piece of real research worth knowing, and it is not about cards — it is about paper.</p>
<p>In 2015 the paper manufacturer Sappi commissioned neuroscientist Dr. David Eagleman to test how the medium changes the message. Participants read the same brochure for the same fictitious company three ways: on high-quality coated paper, on lower-grade uncoated stock, and on a screen. <strong>The people who read it on premium paper understood and remembered the most, formed the best impression of the company, and were the most likely to recommend it.</strong> A week later, brand-name recall among that group ran roughly three to one over the others.</p>
<p>Worth stating plainly: Sappi sells paper. Read it with that in mind. But the study named its researcher and its method, which is more than the circulating card statistics can manage.</p>
<p>There is a separate, older line of work on weight and judgment — hand someone a heavier clipboard and they rate the survey clipped to it as more important. That does not produce a number to print. It explains a mechanism every printer already knows: <strong>people decide how they feel about a card in the two seconds before they read it.</strong></p>

<h2>The specs that actually change how a card feels</h2>
<p>Four decisions do almost all the work. None of them are design decisions.</p>
<p><strong>Thickness.</strong> Standard cards run 14pt. Moving to 16pt is the largest perceptual jump available for the smallest money — the card stops flexing when it is picked up, and that single change is what people read as "this firm is established." Beyond 16pt you are into 32pt duplex territory, which is a different statement and a different budget. A standard run of 250 double-sided cards starts around $60, and finishes and heavier stocks move up from there.</p>
<p><strong>Coating.</strong> Gloss photographs well and is the most common finish, which is worth knowing when you want to be remembered. Matte reads quieter and takes a pen. Soft-touch laminate is the one people comment on out loud, and it costs less than most owners expect. It is the finish most often described as "expensive" without anyone being able to say why.</p>
<p><strong>Edge.</strong> A painted edge is a stripe of color on the 16pt edge of the card. It is invisible in a photograph and unmissable in a hand, and it is the cheapest way to make a card look like it came from somewhere specific.</p>
<p><strong>Embellishment.</strong> Foil, spot UV, blind emboss. Each one directs the eye to exactly one element — which means you have to choose the element. There is peer-reviewed eye-tracking work on whether decorative foil changes where attention lands on a package - Hurley et al., Clemson University, <em>Journal of Applied Packaging Research</em>, 172 participants in an immersive retail lab. It measured packaging on a shelf, not cards in a hand, so it tells us the effect is real enough to measure rather than what foil is worth on a card. The practical rule is simpler than the research: <a href="https://www.alphagraphics.com/us-texas-san-antonio-us769/print-services/business-cards">finish is not decoration</a>, it is where you spend attention.</p>

<h2>The back of the card is the cheapest square inch you own</h2>
<p>It is usually blank. It is already paid for. It is the only part of your marketing budget where the incremental cost of saying something is zero.</p>
<p>What belongs there depends on what you sell. A single line naming the three things you actually do. A QR code to a booking page rather than a homepage. A short list of service areas. A map pin. For a trade, the license number. The test is simple: if someone found the card in a drawer six months from now, what one thing would make them keep it instead of throwing it away?</p>

<h2>How to spec a card</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Decide what the card is for.</strong> A reminder of a conversation, or a first impression with a stranger? The first can be quiet. The second cannot.</li>
<li><strong>Start at 16pt.</strong> Then decide whether anything else is worth adding. Most cards are underweight and over-designed.</li>
<li><strong>Pick one embellishment, not three.</strong> Foil and spot UV and emboss on the same card cancel each other out — there is no longer a single place for the eye to land.</li>
<li><strong>Use the back.</strong> Write the one sentence before you approve the front.</li>
<li><strong>Proof on the actual stock.</strong> Soft-touch, uncoated, and gloss shift color differently. A screen proof will not tell you that.</li>
<li><strong>Hold it before you order 500.</strong> We will cut you a blank in the stock you are considering. Carry it in your pocket for a day and see what you think of it.</li>

<h2>Related questions</h2>
<h3>Do business cards still matter in 2026?</h3>
<p>Yes, in the specific situation they were built for: one person, one hand, one conversation worth continuing. A card is the only piece of marketing a prospect physically keeps, and physical objects survive in ways that a saved contact does not. What has changed is the standard — a thin, glossy, template card now reads as a default rather than a decision, which is why stock and finish carry more weight than they did a decade ago.</p>
<h3>What thickness should a business card be?</h3>
<p>14pt is standard and 16pt is the upgrade worth making. The jump from 14pt to 16pt is the largest change in how a card feels for the smallest change in price — the card stops flexing when it is picked up, which is what people read as substance. 32pt duplex, where two sheets are bonded with a colored core, is a deliberate statement rather than an upgrade and costs accordingly.</p>
<h3>How much do business cards cost?</h3>
<p>A standard run of 250 double-sided cards starts around $60, and finishes and heavier stocks move up from there. Price per card falls steeply with quantity, so 500 rarely costs twice what 250 does. Soft-touch laminate, painted edges, foil, and spot UV are each priced as an add-on to the run rather than folded into a per-card rate, which means you can see exactly what each decision costs before you commit. Bring us the quantity and the finish you are considering and we will quote both ways.</p>
<h3>Is soft-touch coating worth the extra cost?</h3>
<p>It is the finish people comment on without being able to name it. Soft-touch laminate gives the card a velvet surface that reads as expensive, and it pairs particularly well with spot UV because the gloss contrast becomes tactile as well as visual. The tradeoff is that soft-touch will not take a pen, so if people write on the back of your cards, choose uncoated or matte instead.</p>
<h3>What is a painted edge on a business card?</h3>
<p>A painted edge is a band of color applied to the cut edge of a thick card, usually 16pt or heavier. It does not photograph, which is why it rarely appears in online galleries, and it is unmistakable in the hand. On a dark card with a metallic edge it is the single most cost-effective way to signal that the card was specified rather than ordered.</p>
<h3>Does paper quality actually affect how people perceive a business?</h3>
<p>There is research supporting it, though not about cards specifically. In a 2015 study conducted by Dr. David Eagleman for Sappi, readers of the same brochure printed on premium coated paper understood and remembered more, formed a better impression of the company, and recalled its name roughly three to one over readers of an uncoated version or a screen a week later. Sappi manufactures paper, which should be weighed, but the study named its method and its researcher.</p>
<h3>What should go on the back of a business card?</h3>
<p>Something. The back is already printed on and already paid for, and it is usually blank. A single line naming what you actually do, a QR code pointing at a booking page rather than a homepage, service areas, or a license number all outperform white space. Ask what would make someone keep the card if they found it in a drawer six months later.</p>
<h3>How many business cards should I order?</h3>
<p>Enough that you stop rationing them, and no more than the information will outlive. Per-card cost drops with quantity, so the gap between 250 and 500 is usually small in dollars. The better question is when the card will next be wrong: reprint when the information changes, not when you get bored of the design. A run of 250 good cards beats 500 you are not proud to hand over.</p>
